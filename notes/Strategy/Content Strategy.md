# Content Strategy — Tom Stagl

What to write, where to put it, how often, and what the first pieces should be. Built from the Competitive Scan and Audience Map. Tuned for keep-warm mode: low cadence, high quality, no forced schedule.

---

## The Strategic Frame

**Write for peer engineering leaders. Be discovered by executive recruiters.**

Content circulates among Segment B (peer leaders in Rands Slack, ELC, LeadDev). That body of work — even 4-6 pieces per year — builds the reputation layer that Segment A (executive recruiters) finds when they Google Tom's name after sourcing him on LinkedIn.

The content goal is not reach. It is credibility. One piece that gets shared in Rands Slack or cited by another engineering leader is worth more than 100 impressions on a mediocre LinkedIn post.

**The quality bar:** Would a VP Engineering at a comparable company share this link in their leadership Slack? Would someone bookmark it and come back to it? If not, don't publish it.

---

## Content Pillars

Three pillars. Each is grounded in Tom's specific operational experience — not in what the audience wants to read generically, but in what Tom is actually doing and has actually observed. That's the only durable source of content that doesn't feel forced.

---

### Pillar 1 — Engineering at Scale

**The angle:** What running a 350-person engineering unit actually looks like from the inside. Org design, decision-making, what changes at 50 vs 150 vs 350 people, what conventional wisdom is wrong at this scale.

**Why this is Tom's to write:** Very few engineering leaders at this scale write publicly. The conversation on org design is dominated by startup contexts (10-50 engineers) or by consultants one step removed from the problem. Tom is inside the problem.

**Topics to draw from:**
- How teams are structured at 350 people and how that structure evolved (what worked, what broke)
- The span-of-control problem: what it takes to manage engineering leaders effectively vs. managing engineers directly
- When to centralise vs. distribute an engineering function — and how you know you got it wrong
- How engineering strategy gets made in a large unit — and how often the plan is wrong
- What makes a good Sr. Director/VP of Engineering and why it's hard to hire (from someone who has tried)
- The honest account of a specific decision that didn't go as planned — what happened, what was learned

**Tone:** Practitioner speaking to practitioner. Not "here are principles." More: "here's what happened when we tried X."

---

### Pillar 2 — Developer Experience as Strategy

**The angle:** Building the internal tooling and platform layer that engineers depend on — not as an infrastructure cost centre but as a strategic investment with measurable outcomes. What actually moves the needle on developer productivity and what doesn't.

**Why this is Tom's to write:** His unit *builds this.* He is not observing developer experience from the business side; he is the engineering leader responsible for the tools 2,000+ engineers use every day. That operational vantage point is rare and directly relevant to the most-discussed topic in engineering leadership in 2025-2026 (the AI productivity paradox).

**Topics to draw from:**
- What "developer productivity" actually means at the org level vs. the individual level — and why the metrics are often measuring different things
- The AI productivity paradox from inside: what actually changed (and didn't) when AI coding assistants arrived at scale
- How to make the business case for platform engineering investment — the arguments that work and the ones that don't
- What a mature internal developer platform looks like in practice, and what the growing pain stages looked like before you got there
- The measurement problem: which DX metrics are load-bearing and which are noise
- Why developer experience is a retention and hiring problem, not just a speed problem

**Tone:** Grounded in evidence and experience. Admit what you don't know. The audience is smart enough to distrust clean narratives.

---

### Pillar 3 — The Engineering Leadership Arc

**The angle:** What the career trajectory from engineer → engineering leader actually looks like over 20+ years — the transitions, the mistakes, the things that compound. Not career advice. Operational memoir.

**Why this is Tom's to write:** The arc from SCRUM practitioner in 2003 to CTO to enterprise Sr. Director is unusual and specific. Tom has navigated the transition from technical contributor to technical leader to org leader multiple times across different company sizes. That perspective compounds in value as the audience faces their own transitions.

**Topics to draw from:**
- What the transition from technical leader to org leader actually demands — and what you lose
- What it took to be a CTO at a startup vs. what it takes to be a Sr. Director at an enterprise — how different the job actually is
- What Tom wishes he'd known at Director level that he understands now at Sr. Director
- What's changed in engineering leadership over 20 years (and what hasn't)
- The role of personal technical credibility as you move further from the code — how you maintain it, whether you should
- What "engineering leadership at scale" means vs. "managing engineers" — where the line is

**Tone:** Honest and specific. First-person without being navel-gazing. The test: would a peer who has been on this journey recognise something true in it?

## What to Avoid

These patterns were identified in the Competitive Scan as low-value or actively harmful for this audience:

- **Listicles and frameworks.** "5 things great engineering leaders do" has been written. Don't compete there.
- **Success theater.** "We did X and everything went great" — the audience distrusts this and won't share it. Include what went wrong, what was uncertain, what you'd do differently.
- **AI hype without grounded experience.** "AI will transform engineering" as an argument without specific operational evidence. The audience has had enough of this.
- **LinkedIn-native emotional content.** The "I cried when my engineer quit" genre. It reads as performing for an algorithm, not communicating with a peer.
- **Generic "authenticity."** Writing that is warm and human-sounding but contains no specific operational claim. Content that could have been written by anyone.
- **Company-adjacent content.** Reposting Dynatrace's press releases with a personal take bolted on. The brand is Tom's, not Dynatrace's.

## Channels and Format

### Primary: tomstagl.com blog

**The case:** Owned, permanent, portable. Whatever LinkedIn does or doesn't do, the blog post is at tomstagl.com forever. Content can be syndicated to LinkedIn later. The site already exists.

**Format guidance:**
- Length: 800–2,000 words. Long enough to develop an argument, short enough that a senior leader will actually read it.
- Structure: Open with a specific observation or problem — not a thesis statement. End with an honest take, not a prescription. Avoid headers-as-outline structure; write in prose that builds an argument.
- No abstract openers ("In today's rapidly changing landscape..."). Start in the middle of the thing.
- One idea per post. Do not try to be comprehensive. Say one thing clearly.

### Secondary: LinkedIn (cross-post or standalone)

**The case:** The highest-reach surface for peer engineering leaders. A substantive post — not just a link share — gets material engagement with the right audience.

**How to use it:**
- Cross-post adapted versions of blog content. Don't just share the link; give the post legs of its own with 200-400 words and a link at the end.
- Occasional standalone observations: brief, specific, grounded. A one-paragraph take on something happening in engineering leadership right now. Not "thoughts?" — just the take.
- One post per month is sufficient. More than that starts to look like the algorithm is doing the writing.

### Future: Conference speaking (Phase 2 or later)

**The case:** One recorded conference talk at the right venue has more shelf life than 12 months of social posts. LeadDev, ELC Annual, or PlatformCon are the right venues for this audience. Speaking also directly serves Segment A (recruiters who search speakers from known venues).

**Timing:** Not a Phase 1 priority. The blog archive needs to exist first — a CFP for LeadDev or ELC is much stronger with 3-4 existing published pieces that demonstrate the point of view.

**What not to do:** Newsletter. The sustain risk is too high for keep-warm mode. A newsletter that launches and then goes quiet for 4 months reads as worse than no newsletter. Revisit this only if there's genuine appetite for quarterly-minimum cadence.

## Cadence

**Target: 4–6 posts per year.** One every 6-10 weeks.

This is not a schedule to keep. It is a floor that prevents the brand from going dormant. In keep-warm mode, the goal is to ensure that at any point when someone lands on the site or searches Tom's name, there is evidence of recent activity — not a blog last updated in 2023.

**Realistic cadence model:**
- A good quarter: two blog posts + one LinkedIn adaptation each
- A quiet quarter: one post + LinkedIn adaptation
- A break quarter: nothing new, but the archive is there doing passive work

**When to write:** After a project wraps, a decision gets made, a conversation surfaces something that hasn't been articulated yet. Not on a Monday morning because the calendar says "content week."

**The trigger question:** "Would my peer VP Engineering at a different company find this useful and specific enough to share?" If yes, write it. If the answer is "maybe," write it anyway and the edit will tell you.

## Anchor Content — First Three Pieces

These are the pieces to write first. They establish the pillars and give the archive something to stand on. Ordered by current relevance and ease of access to Tom's direct experience.

### Piece 1 — Pillar 2

**Working title:** *"What actually changed (and didn't) when we gave 2,000 engineers AI coding assistants"*

**Why first:** The AI productivity paradox is the dominant conversation in engineering leadership right now. Tom is building the tooling layer where this plays out. He has direct operational observation that almost no one else publishing on this topic has. This is the piece most likely to circulate in Rands Slack, get picked up by The Pragmatic Engineer, or get Tom a speaking invitation.

**The argument to make:** An honest account of what the measurements showed, what surprised us, what didn't work as expected, and what we're still figuring out. Not "here's how to adopt AI" — that's been written. More: "here's what actually happened, warts and all."

**Length:** 1,200–1,800 words.

---

### Piece 2 — Pillar 1

**Working title:** *"What breaks in an engineering org between 50 and 350 people"*

**Why second:** Org design at scale is perennially relevant and almost always written by people who are theorizing from outside the scale. Tom is inside it. The specific number (350) is itself credibility. This piece builds the Pillar 1 archive and positions Tom as a practitioner of engineering at a specific scale.

**The argument to make:** Choose one or two specific things that broke or had to be redesigned as the unit grew — structure, decision-making, communication, how work gets prioritized. Ground it in specific episodes, not general lessons.

**Length:** 1,000–1,500 words.

---

### Piece 3 — Pillar 3

**Working title:** *"What the CTO job taught me that the Director job didn't"* (or similar)

**Why third:** The career arc from startup CTO to enterprise Sr. Director is unusual and the lessons cut both ways — what startup contexts teach that enterprise doesn't, and vice versa. This piece is evergreen (not tied to a current hot topic) and establishes the personal authority layer that makes the other pillars more credible.

**The argument to make:** One specific thing the CTO experience gave him that changed how he operates now — or one thing he had to unlearn. Not a retrospective list. One thing, explored honestly.

**Length:** 800–1,200 words. Shorter is fine for this one.

## How the Strategy Evolves

The Content Strategist runs again after:
- 6 months and 3+ posts published (check: are the pillars resonating? what's getting shared?)
- A meaningful shift in Tom's role or the market context
- Before shifting from keep-warm to active mode

When mode shifts to active, the cadence increases and a conference talk becomes a priority. The pillar structure stays; the frequency and amplification effort scales up.
