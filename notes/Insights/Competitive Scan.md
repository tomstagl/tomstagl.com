# Competitive Scan: Engineering Leader Personal Brand

Working document. Real examples, direct observations, gaps noted.

---

## 1. Who Has Strong "Keep Warm" Engineering Leader Brands

These are senior engineering leaders with credible public presence who are not full-time content creators. What they share: a specific and consistent point of view, substantive writing (not posting noise), and a primary home base (blog or site) backed by selective presence elsewhere.

**Will Larson — [lethain.com](https://lethain.com)**
CTO at Carta, previously Stripe, Uber, Calm. Site has been running since at least 2010. Writes about org design, staff eng careers, engineering strategy. Has written four books (*An Elegant Puzzle*, *Staff Engineer*, *The Engineering Executive's Primer*, *Crafting Engineering Strategy*). Low posting frequency — maybe a post every 2-4 weeks — but each piece is substantive and gets wide circulation on HN and among eng leaders. Active on Mastodon and Bluesky but not loud about it. His own take: he wrote a piece called ["Building personal and organizational prestige"](https://lethain.com/building-prestige/) arguing that **prestige** (passive recognition) matters more than **brand** (active narrative), and that most successful executives he knows don't write publicly at all. That's a useful counterweight to the "post constantly" advice elsewhere.

**Michael Lopp (Rands) — [randsinrepose.com](https://randsinrepose.com)**
VP Eng at Slack, then Sr Director at Apple. Writing since 2002 under the pen name "Rands." Books include *Managing Humans* and *Being Geek*. Classic example of a long-running blog that became a durable brand asset. Posts irregularly, but the archive is the asset. Shows that writing quality and specificity compounds over time — the body of work outlasts any individual post. Very little promotional machinery around it.

**Charity Majors — [charity.wtf](https://charity.wtf)**
Co-founder and CTO of Honeycomb.io. Writes about observability, operations, engineering culture, developer experience. Co-authored *Observability Engineering* (O'Reilly). Her voice is direct and opinionated — often contrarian, sometimes abrasive. Has a strong Twitter/X presence (though that platform has degraded). The site itself is plainly built. What works: she writes with genuine conviction about a specific technical domain she helped define, and doesn't pretend to be neutral. Active speaker at LeadDev and similar conferences.

**Addy Osmani — [addyosmani.com](https://addyosmani.com)**
Now Director at Google Cloud AI, previously Head of Chrome Developer Experience. Long track record of technical writing, open source (Yeoman, TodoMVC), and books (*Image Optimization*, *Learning JavaScript Design Patterns*). Site is a clean personal domain. Writing is primarily technical with occasional leadership reflection. Presence at conferences (SmashingConf, etc). Good example of a leader who kept a credible technical brand through role transitions.

**Sarah Drasner — [engmanagement.dev](https://www.engmanagement.dev)**
Director of Engineering at Google (Core Developer Web). Previously VP Engineering at Netlify. Published *Engineering Management for the Rest of Us* (self-published book with dedicated domain). Speaker at conferences. Her brand is differentiated by the book, not volume of posting. The book anchors everything else. Low ongoing posting cadence.

**Anjuan Simmons — [anjuansimmons.com](https://anjuansimmons.com)**
Engineering leader (Help Scout, etc). Featured in First Round's ["Engineering Leader's Guide to Crafting a Personal Brand"](https://review.firstround.com/the-engineering-leaders-guide-to-crafting-a-personal-brand-that-stands-out-from-the-crowd/). Builds brand through public speaking and a personal blog. Known for a specific talk ("Lending Privilege"). Good example of someone who built a credible brand through one strong angle without being a full-time creator.

**Lena Reinhard — [lenareinhard.com](https://www.lenareinhard.com)**
Ex-VP Engineering at CircleCI and Travis CI, now a leadership coach. Personal site with articles and newsletter. Writes on engineering leadership in high-change environments. Has moved from operator to advisor, so her brand work is more deliberate than most, but the output level is still moderate — she's not flooding inboxes. Featured at LeadDev and CTO Craft.

**Kelsey Hightower — Google Cloud**
Principal Developer Advocate at Google. Primarily known through conference speaking and demo work rather than a personal blog or newsletter. Strong GitHub presence. Example of a leader whose brand is almost entirely built through speaking and community participation — no blog, no newsletter, just consistent in-person presence and open source work. Arguably the purest "prestige without brand" case.

### What they have in common

- A **specific point of view** — not "here are 5 leadership tips" but an actual argument or domain claim. Majors on observability. Larson on staff engineering and org design. Drasner on the human side of management. Osmani on developer experience and web performance.
- A **stable home base** (personal domain, not just LinkedIn or Twitter). The domain is theirs regardless of employer.
- **Low but consistent output**. None of them are posting daily. Most are publishing something every few weeks or slower. Quality over frequency.
- **Writing that is worth bookmarking**. The benchmark seems to be: would someone share this link in a Slack channel 6 months from now? Not: would this get likes today.
- **Some form of durable artifact** — a book, a talk on YouTube, an article series — that circulates independently of new publishing activity.
- **Zero job-hunting energy**. None of these sites have "available for opportunities" language. The signal is confident incumbency, not availability.

## 2. Content Pillars That Work

Based on what the above leaders write about and what gets traction in engineering leadership communities (LeadDev, HN, engineering Slack groups):

**Topics that land**

- Org design and team structure (how to organize large engineering orgs, when to centralize vs distribute, platform team models)
- Engineering effectiveness and developer productivity (what actually moves the needle vs vanity metrics)
- The staff/principal/distinguished engineer career path (Larson basically defined this conversation)
- Engineering strategy — connecting technical decisions to business outcomes
- Hiring and leveling (what does a strong Sr Dir/VP actually look for, what's broken about interviews)
- Lessons from failures and near-misses (specific, not vague — "we shipped the wrong thing for 18 months because of X")
- The manager's job in a world of AI-assisted engineering (what changes, what doesn't)
- Platform engineering and internal developer platforms as strategic decisions, not just ops

**Topics that feel try-hard or marketing-speak**

- "Here are 5 qualities of great leaders" listicles — generic, has been written a thousand times
- Posts about how you hired great people and everything went well — success theater
- AI hype without grounded experience — "AI will transform engineering" without actually saying how or with what tradeoffs
- Over-sharing emotional workplace moments in a way that reads as LinkedIn-native (the "I cried when my engineer quit" genre)
- Posting engagement bait ("Agree or disagree?") — technical audiences find this contemptible
- Corporate messaging recycled as personal insight ("At [company], we believe in...")
- Reposting your company's press release with a personal take bolted on

**The quality bar that matters for this audience**

Engineers and eng leaders share things that are specific, counter-intuitive, or built on observed evidence. The test is whether the reader learns something they didn't already know. Generic "authenticity" content or soft leadership advice without operational specificity tends to get scrolled past.

## 3. "Keep Warm" vs "Actively Job-Hunting" — The Signals

This is one of the cleaner distinctions in the research.

**Keep-warm brand signals**
- Writes about their current domain and current role context ("at the scale we operate...")
- Shares opinions on industry trends from the perspective of someone already doing the work
- Profile/site has a clear current role and doesn't mention openness to opportunities
- Occasional posts, high quality, no schedule visible
- Engages with peer community (comments, amplifies others) without calling attention to it
- Long-term archive of writing that shows consistent thinking over time

**Actively job-hunting signals** (to avoid)
- "Open to Work" banner (obvious)
- Sudden spike in posting frequency after a long silence
- Posts that emphasize transferable skills and career highlights rather than craft and domain thinking
- LinkedIn profile rewritten to lead with outcomes and impact metrics rather than what they actually do
- Networking posts that are transparently about positioning ("So excited to connect with so many amazing leaders lately...")
- Content that's suspiciously broad — suddenly writing about many unrelated topics rather than one core domain
- The About section reads like a cover letter

The First Round piece is useful here: it frames building a brand as something you do as part of being a practitioner, not as a job search activity. The output is a byproduct of doing the work and having views, not a calculated visibility campaign.

Will Larson's prestige framing is also relevant: the most valuable brand signal for a senior eng leader isn't "look at me" content — it's the background signal of having shipped things, worked at known companies, and had your work referenced by others. That doesn't require active brand management.

## 4. Platforms — Signal-to-Noise for This Goal

Ranked by relevance for a "keep warm" engineering leadership brand:

**LinkedIn — highest ROI for effort, unavoidable**

For an enterprise engineering leader, LinkedIn is the primary operating surface. This is where board members, peer VPs, executive recruiters, and potential future employers actually look. The profile alone does a lot of work without any active posting. Key elements: current role stated clearly, About section that reads like a professional peer wrote it (not HR), specific accomplishments with context, and recommendations from credible peers. Posting is optional but occasional substantive posts get material reach among the right people. The algorithm in 2025-2026 rewards dwell time and real comments over likes. One solid post per month beats ten shallow ones.

**Personal site / blog — durable, owns the asset**

The case for a personal domain is not reach, it's ownership and permanence. LinkedIn can change, Substack can change, Twitter already has changed. A personal domain is the stable home base everything else can point to. Doesn't need to be updated constantly — an archive of 10-15 strong pieces is more valuable than a high-posting-frequency site with mediocre content. Tom's existing site (tomstagl.com) is well-positioned for this.

**Newsletter (Substack, LinkedIn newsletter) — optional but compounding**

A newsletter signals commitment and builds a direct relationship with subscribers who opted in. For a "keep warm" goal with low cadence requirements, even a quarterly newsletter is viable. The risk: inconsistency looks worse than not having one. Don't start a newsletter you won't sustain. If started, Substack gives portability that LinkedIn newsletters don't.

**Speaking — high leverage but high effort**

One recorded conference talk has longer shelf life than 100 social posts. LeadDev, QCon, PlatformCon, internal engineering conferences — these reach exactly the right audience. But speaking requires significant preparation time. Worth pursuing selectively (once or twice a year) at the right venue rather than grinding through low-tier events.

**Twitter/X — declining signal, not worth prioritizing**

X has degraded significantly as a professional platform. Many eng leaders have migrated or gone quiet. Will Larson, for example, is now more active on Mastodon and Bluesky. For a "keep warm" brand goal, investing time in X in 2026 has poor ROI. The audience has fragmented.

**Bluesky — emerging but niche**

Growing among the technical community, with a more signal-heavy feed. Worth maintaining a handle and occasional presence, but not the primary venue. If the tech community continues migrating here it matters more over the next 2 years.

**GitHub — passive but real**

Public repo activity (stars, contributions, projects) is read by technically sophisticated recruiters and peers. A neglected or empty GitHub profile reads as absence. Doesn't require active effort — just not deleting things.

## 5. Biggest Gaps and Mistakes

These are the patterns that show up repeatedly across sources and in the examples above:

**Mistake 1: No home base / everything is on borrowed land**

Leaders who only post on LinkedIn or Twitter have no portable asset. When they change jobs, get laid off, or the platform degrades, the brand evaporates. A personal domain is the minimal viable foundation.

**Mistake 2: Starting loud and going silent**

Beginning a newsletter or posting campaign and then going quiet for 6 months is worse than never starting. It signals lack of follow-through, and it's exactly what happens when the brand effort is triggered by a job search rather than genuine practice. The "keep warm" framing argues for low-but-steady over burst-and-fade.

**Mistake 3: Writing for generic audiences instead of peer audiences**

Engineering leader personal brand writing often defaults to "5 leadership lessons" content aimed at managers generally, which competes with thousands of similar pieces and attracts the wrong audience. The higher-value move is writing for peers — people at the same or slightly higher level — on topics that require operational experience to address. That's a far smaller but far more valuable audience.

**Mistake 4: Hiding behind the company brand**

Senior leaders often conflate their company's visibility with their own. Dynatrace has a brand; Tom Stagl has a separate personal brand that should exist independently. If Tom left Dynatrace tomorrow, the company brand goes with the job. What remains is the personal domain, the writing archive, the speaking record, and the peer relationships. That's what needs building.

**Mistake 5: Treating brand as a job-search tool rather than a career insurance policy**

The moment brand-building is triggered by an employment threat, all the patterns of desperation become legible. Profiles get rewritten, posting spikes, the content is suddenly about transferable skills. Experienced executives and recruiters read this instantly. The "keep warm" strategy works precisely because it's built over time without urgency — so that when urgency arrives, the foundation is already there.

**Mistake 6: Not having a specific point of view**

The weakest brand positioning is "experienced engineering leader who cares about people, process, and technology." That describes almost everyone. The strongest positioning owns a specific tension or insight: Majors on the failure modes of traditional monitoring, Larson on what it actually takes to operate at staff level, Rands on the weird psychological reality of managing technical people. The specific POV is what makes content shareable and what makes someone memorable in a search.

**Mistake 7: Undervaluing the profile and overvaluing the posting**

LinkedIn profile optimization (headline, About, experience descriptions, recommendations) gets orders of magnitude more passive views than posts. For a low-effort, high-return investment, the profile is it. Most leaders have profiles that read like a resume dump rather than a credible professional positioning statement.

## Relevance to Tom

Tom's natural positioning zone given the above: he runs a 350-person engineering unit building internal tooling for 2000+ engineers at an enterprise software company. That's platform engineering / developer experience at serious scale — a domain that has concrete traction right now. He also has a long arc: SCRUM pioneer from 2003, CTO, multiple Director roles. That kind of trajectory is rare and specific enough to be genuinely interesting to the peer audience.

The gap right now: the personal site exists but the writing archive doesn't. There's a positioning statement but not a consistent POV. No newsletter, no talks in the public record.

Minimal viable "keep warm" foundation based on this scan:
1. Clean LinkedIn profile that reads as confident operator, not job candidate
2. Personal site with an About that reflects current engineering leadership positioning (done)
3. 4-6 substantive pieces of writing on specific topics from operational experience — these can go on the site and/or LinkedIn
4. One or two speaking appearances at the right venues over the next 12-18 months
5. A Substack or LinkedIn newsletter only if there's genuine appetite to sustain it at low cadence
