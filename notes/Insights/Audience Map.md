# Audience Map — Tom Stagl

Who needs to find Tom, where they are, what they read, and what they're trying to solve. Written to inform content strategy and channel decisions for a "keep warm" engineering leadership brand.

---

## 1. Primary Audience Segments

### Segment A — Executive Recruiters & Talent Scouts

**Who they are:** Retained search partners and in-house talent acquisition teams at tech companies and enterprise software firms filling VP Engineering, Sr. Director, and CTO-track roles. They are actively searching for candidates who aren't looking.

**What triggers their interest in someone:**
- Accurate, senior title on LinkedIn headline (the single most-searched field)
- Employment at known company signals scale and calibre — Dynatrace is a recognised name
- Visible progression within one employer (promotions over time = retained trust, not just tenure)
- Personal site or writing presence signals self-direction and professional seriousness
- Peer referrals from other engineering leaders they already know

**How they find candidates:**
- LinkedIn Recruiter keyword searches: title, company, location, years of experience. ~95% of executive recruiters use LinkedIn as their primary discovery tool.
- Google name search to qualify candidates — everything public is visible and is reviewed
- Referrals: they ask peer engineers and executives "who should we talk to for this role?"
- Occasionally: blog posts or conference talks that surface someone as a known operator in a specific domain

**Their pain:**
Finding engineering leaders who have genuinely run engineering at scale (not just managed teams), who aren't visibly job-searching (desperation is a disqualifier at senior levels), and who have a credible track record across multiple phases of a company's growth.

**What this audience needs from Tom's brand:**
- LinkedIn headline that is accurate and keyword-rich: Senior Director, Software Engineering — not "Director Software Development"
- About section that reads as confident operator, not cover letter
- Promotion trajectory visible: three separate Dynatrace roles, not one collapsed entry
- A personal site that exists and is professionally current (they look for this)

---

### Segment B — Peer Engineering Leaders (VPs, Sr. Directors, CTOs)

**Who they are:** Engineering leaders at mid-to-large technology companies, enterprise software firms, and scale-ups. They run units of 50–500+ engineers. They have genuine operational complexity to navigate and are looking for peers who think clearly about the same problems.

**Where they live online:**
- **Rands Leadership Slack** — 30,000+ members, the largest async community for engineering leadership. Discussion spans org design, career development, tool choices, leadership philosophy. Quality is high because the community polices noise.
- **ELC (Engineering Leadership Community)** — 8,000+ members from 3,000+ companies. Peer group programs, curated; tends toward Directors, VPs, and above.
- **EngineeringX** — hundreds of CTOs and VPs specifically focused on developer experience and engineering productivity. Directly relevant to Tom's domain.
- **CTO Connection** — global, vetted, senior-only (manual review). For the CTO/VP tier specifically.
- **LinkedIn** — not primarily a community, but where they maintain presence and occasionally engage with content from peers they respect.
- **Hacker News** — eng leaders read HN and occasionally post; the "Ask HN: how do you manage..." threads get high-signal responses from practitioners. Not a community to post into, but a distribution surface for content that's link-worthy.
- **Bluesky** — growing engineering community after Twitter/X migration. Will Larson and others have moved here. Not yet the default but gaining signal density.

**What they read:**
- **The Pragmatic Engineer** (Gergely Orosz) — #1 tech newsletter on Substack, 10M+ podcast downloads in 2025. The standard reference point for anyone who follows the industry.
- **Irrational Exuberance** (Will Larson, lethain.com) — deeply influential in the Sr. Director/VP/CTO tier for org design, staff engineering, and engineering strategy.
- **Rands in Repose** (Michael Lopp) — long-running, archives are the asset. High trust among managers who have been reading for years.
- **LeadDev** — articles, conference talks, and recordings. Audience spans EM through VP. LeadingEng track is specifically for Sr. Directors and above.
- **ELC Newsletter & Podcast** — curated for CTOs, VPs, Directors.
- **Pat Kua's newsletter** — engineering leadership strategy, org dynamics.
- **The Engineering Manager** (James Stanier) — practical management content, strong following.
- **Manager.dev** (Anton Zaides) — hands-on engineering management.
- **Stratechery** (Ben Thompson) — business strategy layer; engineers who think about the business context of their work.

**What they're trying to solve (2025–2026 pain points):**
- **The AI productivity paradox** — 75%+ of developers are using AI coding assistants but main branch throughput is declining (one study shows -6.8% even as feature branch throughput rose +15%). Leaders are accountable for AI spend and are being asked to show measurable ROI. Most organisations have AI usage driven by bottom-up experimentation with no structure, governance, or measurement.
- **Technical debt acceleration** — AI-generated code is creating debt faster than teams can pay it down. Code review and quality processes designed for human-paced output are breaking.
- **Delivery pipeline bottlenecks** — The constraint has shifted from "writing code" to "shipping code safely." CI/CD infrastructure isn't keeping up with AI-accelerated development.
- **Org design in AI-augmented teams** — what does a team of 10 engineers look like when each one is effectively 2x more productive? How do you structure, staff, and level a team that looks fundamentally different in 12 months?
- **Developer burnout** — expectations have risen faster than capacity. AI has increased pressure without proportionally reducing load.
- **Measuring engineering effectiveness** — DORA metrics, SPACE, DORA+AI — the measurement landscape is fragmented. Leaders are being held accountable for numbers they don't fully trust.

**What makes content worth sharing in this community:**
- Specific, not generic. "We built X and here's what actually happened" beats "here's a framework for thinking about X."
- Counter-intuitive or admits tradeoffs. The audience is experienced enough to distrust anything that sounds too clean.
- Grounded in observable evidence — not "AI will change everything" but "we measured this and here's what we saw."
- Written by someone who is clearly doing the work, not narrating it from the outside.
- Passes the "would I share this in Rands Slack / with my CPTO?" test — that's the quality bar.

### Segment C — Aspiring Engineering Leaders (Senior Engineers, Engineering Managers)

**Who they are:** Strong individual contributors considering management, and early/mid-career engineering managers looking for frameworks and role models. They're 5–10 years behind Tom in trajectory.

**Where they live online:** LeadDev (the core audience), The Pragmatic Engineer, StaffPlus conference (for Sr. ICs), Rands Leadership Slack lower tiers, Twitter/Bluesky.

**What they read:** LeadDev articles, Staff Engineer by Will Larson, An Elegant Puzzle by Will Larson, The Manager's Path by Camille Fournier, Engineering Management for the Rest of Us by Sarah Drasner.

**What they're solving:** How to transition from IC to manager, how to level up, what it looks like to run engineering at scale, what mistakes to avoid. They're pattern-matching against people who have done it.

**Relevance to Tom's brand:** Secondary audience. Not the hiring trigger, but they amplify good content. Someone who was a junior EM 3 years ago and now runs a 50-person team remembers and references the leaders who wrote things they found useful. This is the "prestige over time" dynamic — building a reputation with people who will eventually be your peers.

**What this audience needs from Tom:** Honest accounts of what it's actually like to run engineering at scale, what he got wrong, what surprised him. Not career advice — operational specificity.

---

### Segment D — Potential Collaborators and Future Clients

**Who they are:** Consultants, advisors, coaches, fractional leaders, and conference organisers who might want to work with or feature Tom. Also: founders and scale-up CTOs who want perspectives from someone who has done enterprise engineering at Tom's scale.

**Relevance now:** Low — not the primary "keep warm" audience. But over time, a coherent brand builds optionality for consulting engagements, advisory roles, or speaking invitations.

**What they need to see:** A clear point of view (not just experience), evidence of thinking publicly, and a home base they can link to.

## 3. What Segment A Actually Searches For (Recruiter Keywords)

When a retained search firm or in-house recruiter looks for someone at Tom's level, they typically filter on:

- **Title keywords:** "VP Engineering," "Senior Director Software Engineering," "Director of Engineering," "Head of Engineering"
- **Company keywords:** Enterprise software, SaaS, B2B tech — Dynatrace is a recognised brand in this space
- **Scale keywords:** Not always searchable, but they look for it in the profile — "at scale," "2,000+ engineers," "platform engineering"
- **Domain keywords:** "Developer experience," "platform engineering," "internal developer platforms," "engineering effectiveness," "developer productivity"
- **Location:** Vienna / Austria / remote-open — relevant for EMEA engineering leadership roles specifically

Tom's LinkedIn profile currently underserves this search because the title is stale ("Director Software Development" vs. actual "Senior Director Software Engineering") and the scale context is absent from the visible summary.

## 4. Relevance of Tom's Domain to Current Audience Interests

Tom's operational context — running a 350-person unit building tooling and infrastructure for 2000+ engineers — sits at the intersection of the hottest topics in engineering leadership right now:

- **Platform engineering** is at ~55% adoption and Gartner forecasts 80% by 2026. Leaders everywhere are figuring out what it means to build an IDP seriously. Tom has been doing this at real scale.
- **AI + developer experience** — the AI productivity paradox is the defining challenge for engineering leaders in 2025-2026. Tom's team is building the tooling layer where AI and developer experience intersect. That's a rare perspective.
- **Engineering at scale** — org design for hundreds of engineers is a topic with very few first-hand practitioners willing to write about it. Most content on org design is theoretical or from a startup context (20-50 engineers). 350 people is a different problem.

This means Tom's content — if written about what he actually does — is not competing with generic management writing. It's filling a gap where practitioners at this scale rarely speak publicly.

## 5. Implications for Strategy

**Write for Segment B, discovered by Segment A.**

The peer engineering leader audience (Segment B) is where content circulates and reputation is built. Writing for them — about platform engineering at scale, AI-augmented engineering org design, measuring developer effectiveness at 2000+ engineers — produces content that gets shared in Rands Slack, cited by other leaders, and surfaces Tom as a credible practitioner.

That same body of work, combined with an accurate LinkedIn profile, is what Executive Recruiters (Segment A) find when they Google Tom's name or land on his site after sourcing him.

**Prioritised actions by audience impact:**

1. **Fix LinkedIn profile** (Segment A — recruiter discoverability): update headline, rewrite About opening, break Dynatrace into three entries. Highest ROI, lowest effort.
2. **2-4 substantive blog posts per year** (Segment B — peer credibility): topics from operational experience at scale. Platform engineering, AI in developer tooling, org design. Not listicles — specific, grounded pieces.
3. **Maintain Rands Slack presence** (Segment B — ambient community): participate when you have something specific to add. Don't perform, just show up when relevant.
4. **One conference talk** (Segments A, B, and D — prestige and referrals): LeadDev, ELC Annual, or PlatformCon. A recorded talk has longer shelf life than most content.
5. **Bluesky handle maintenance** (future optionality): the engineering community is migrating here. Low effort to keep presence warm.

**What to avoid for the "keep warm" goal:**
- Starting a newsletter you won't sustain — inconsistency signals more than silence
- Posting on a cadence that looks like job-searching (sudden volume spike after a long silence)
- Generic leadership content aimed at aspiring managers — it reaches the wrong audience and doesn't differentiate
- Twitter/X investment — signal-to-noise has degraded, audience has fragmented
